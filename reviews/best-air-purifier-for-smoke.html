How Air Purifiers Handle Smoke: HEPA, Carbon and CADR

Smoke is mostly tiny particles. Wildfire smoke and cigarette smoke are both dominated by fine particulate matter — PM2.5, particles smaller than 2.5 microns — which is exactly the size range that lodges deep in the lungs and makes smoke a health problem rather than just a nuisance. That is why the filter type matters so much: a True HEPA filter is rated to capture 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns and larger, which covers the bulk of smoke particles. Filters labelled "HEPA-type" are not held to that standard and should not be trusted for smoke.

But HEPA only catches particles. Smoke also carries gases and volatile compounds — the part you smell — and those pass straight through a HEPA filter. That is the job of activated carbon: a carbon stage adsorbs some gases and odours, which is why every purifier in this guide includes one. Carbon saturates, though, and in heavy smoke it saturates fast, so the smell comes back sooner than the particle protection fades. The US EPA's guide to air cleaners in the home makes the same distinction: filtration targets particles, and no portable air cleaner removes every pollutant from indoor air.

The third piece is CADR — the Clean Air Delivery Rate. Where HEPA tells you what a filter can catch, CADR tells you how much clean air the machine actually delivers into the room, measured independently through AHAM. For smoke, always use the smoke CADR, which is the lowest of the three CADR figures (dust, pollen, smoke) because smoke particles are the hardest to capture.

Smoke CADR: The Number That Decides Whether It Works

Most buyers get this wrong: they compare headline room coverage, which manufacturers calculate generously, instead of smoke CADR. The rule is simple and comes from AHAM itself — the smoke CADR should be at least two-thirds of the room's square footage. A bedroom of 300 square feet needs a smoke CADR of 200 or more. The Coway Mighty's verified smoke CADR of 233 clears that bar with room to spare, which is why it is our top pick for the bedroom — the room where you spend a third of your life breathing.

The Blueair Blue Pure 211i Max sits in the same large-room class, with the added advantage of running remarkably quietly — important, because a smoke purifier has to run continuously, day and night, and a loud machine gets turned off.

The Winix 5500-2 is the quiet achiever of the group. Using a Purifier for Smoke: Placement, Settings and Filter Care

Buying the right purifier is half the job; using it right is the other half. The EPA's guidance is clear on the basics: during a smoke event, keep windows and doors closed, run the purifier continuously on its highest setting you can tolerate, and put it in the room where you spend the most time — usually the bedroom. A purifier on low in a closed bedroom does far more good than a big purifier on high in a draughty hallway.

Placement matters more than most people think. Put the purifier a few feet from the wall with clear air around its intake, not jammed into a corner or behind furniture. If your model has an air-quality sensor and auto mode, let it run on auto — the Coway's sensor reacts to real-time air quality, and the Levoit's PM2.5 display shows you the number falling, which is genuinely reassuring during a smoke event. But on the worst days, override auto and run on high: auto modes are tuned for comfort, not for a wildfire.

Finally, respect the filter. Smoke loads a HEPA filter far faster than dust or pollen, and a clogged filter cuts airflow and CADR exactly when you need them most. During a bad smoke season, check the filter monthly and budget for a replacement every 3–6 months instead of the usual 6–12. Buying a spare filter with the purifier is the cheapest insurance in this category — and it is also the reason to check filter prices before you buy, since a cheap purifier with expensive filters is no bargain.

Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

  1. True HEPA only: avoid "HEPA-type" filters — they are not held to the 99.97% standard.
  2. Match smoke CADR to the room: smoke CADR should be at least two-thirds of the room's square footage.
  3. Check the carbon stage: without activated carbon, the smell of smoke stays even when the particles are gone.
  4. Plan for continuous running: check noise on high — a purifier you switch off is a purifier that does nothing.
  5. Price the filters: smoke seasons burn through filters; a spare filter belongs in the same order.
  6. Buy before smoke season: purifiers sell out during wildfire events — buy in late November sales, not in July.
