How Rowing Works — and Why It Burns So Much

Rowing is one of the few exercises that works almost the whole body in a single movement. Each stroke starts with a leg drive, continues through the hips and core, and finishes with the arms pulling the handle to the chest — legs, glutes, back, shoulders, arms and core all contribute. Because so much muscle is working at once, the heart rate climbs fast and stays high, which is why rowing is such an efficient calorie burner despite being seated and low-impact. The American Council on Exercise notes that rowing delivers a full-body, low-impact workout that works every major muscle group, and that its flywheel design lets you control the intensity — easy cruising or all-out intervals on the same machine.

The two rowers here represent the two philosophies. The Concept2 RowErg is the standard that gyms, rowing clubs and competitive athletes have used for decades: air resistance, a PM5 performance monitor that measures every stroke precisely, and a frame that separates into two pieces for storage. It has no screen classes and no subscription — just you, the flywheel, and the numbers. The Hydrow Wave is the modern connected alternative: a 16-inch touchscreen, electromagnetic drag that feels like water, and trainer-led classes on rivers around the world, with a membership that costs about $50 a month for the whole household. If you need coaching and entertainment to stay consistent, the Hydrow earns its price; if you are self-motivated, the Concept2 costs less and lasts longer. Treadmill vs Rower: Calories, Joints, Muscles and Space

On calories, running has a small edge at the same perceived effort, because running is weight-bearing — you lift your whole body with every stride. But the gap is smaller than most people think, and it disappears if you row hard: a vigorous rowing interval session can match or beat a moderate run. The honest answer is that the machine you use four times a week burns far more than the one gathering dust, so enjoyment matters more than the calorie tables. Treadmills win on simplicity and on bone health — weight-bearing exercise supports bone density — while rowers win on total muscle engagement and joint-friendliness.

On joints, rowing wins clearly. Running sends impact through the knees, ankles and hips with every footstrike; cushioned decks like the Sole F63's help, but they cannot remove impact entirely. Rowing is seated and smooth, which is why it is a common choice for people returning from knee injuries or carrying extra weight. On space, rowing wins again: the RowErg is 96 inches long in use but splits in half for storage, and the Hydrow Wave stores upright with a vertical anchor. Folding treadmills help — both the Sole F63 and the Horizon 7.0 AT fold their decks up — but they remain heavy, tall objects that need a dedicated corner. Which Machine Should You Buy?

Buy a treadmill if you love running, want the most natural workout possible, or are training for a race — nothing replaces road-feel running like a good belt under your feet. The Sole F63 is our pick for most runners: a 3.0 HP motor, a roomy 20 by 60 inch deck, a folding frame, and a lifetime frame and motor warranty, all at a mid-range price. The Horizon 7.0 AT suits interval trainers: its Quick Dial controls change speed and incline instantly without stabbing at buttons, it syncs with Apple Watch and apps like Zwift and Peloton, and its 20 by 60 inch cushioned deck is equally runner-friendly. If you mostly walk, also consider a walking pad — see our best walking pad gifts guide for the compact alternative.

Buy a rower if your knees, ankles or weight make running uncomfortable, if you want one machine that trains your whole body, or if floor space is limited. The Concept2 RowErg is the default recommendation for a reason: it is the benchmark for durability, its monitor data is comparable worldwide, and it holds its resale value better than almost any other home gym equipment. The Hydrow Wave is the right choice if classes and coaching keep you consistent — its 16-inch screen, electromagnetic resistance and 30-day home trial make starting easy, but remember the membership cost on top of the machine price. Either way, start gently: rowing form matters, and five good minutes beat twenty sloppy ones.

Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

  1. Measure the space: a treadmill needs about 7 feet of length; a rower needs 8 feet in use but stores much smaller.
  2. Check the weight limit: the Horizon 7.0 AT supports up to 325 lb; the Concept2 RowErg is rated to 500 lb.
  3. Motor matters for treadmills: look for at least 3.0 HP continuous duty if you plan to run regularly.
  4. Count the subscription: Hydrow membership is about $50 a month — factor it into the true cost.
  5. Try the form first: watch a rowing technique video before your first session to protect your back.
  6. Buy in Black Friday Week: treadmills and rowers see some of their best prices of the year in late November.

At the same effort level they are close, and the winner depends on intensity. Running on a treadmill at a strong pace burns slightly more per minute for most people, because running is weight-bearing. Rowing is close behind while working far more muscle groups, and because rowing is low-impact you can sustain hard efforts longer without joint pain.

Both work for weight loss when paired with a sensible diet, because both let you sustain a high heart rate. Rowing has an edge for people with knee or joint issues, since it is low-impact, and it builds upper-body and core strength at the same time. A treadmill has an edge if you love running and will actually use it every day — consistency beats the machine.

Rowing with good form strengthens the back, but poor form can strain it. Keep your back straight, drive with your legs first, and avoid rounding your shoulders at the catch. People with existing back problems should start gently and consider talking to a doctor or physiotherapist before rowing regularly.
