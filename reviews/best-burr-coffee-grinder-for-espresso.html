What Is a Burr Grinder — and Why Does Espresso Demand One?

A burr grinder crushes coffee beans between two ridged metal surfaces — the burrs — set a precise distance apart. That distance sets the grind size, and because every bean passes through the same gap, the grounds come out far more uniform than anything a blade grinder can produce. A blade grinder, by contrast, chops beans at random: some pieces turn to dust while others stay in chunks. For filter coffee that inconsistency is forgiving; for espresso it is fatal, because water under pressure finds the path of least resistance and channels through the fine dust, over-extracting bitterness while the coarse chunks stay under-extracted and sour.

Espresso is uniquely demanding because the window is so narrow. The water passes through the puck in about 25–30 seconds, so the grind must be fine enough to slow it down but even enough to let it flow uniformly. That is why the adjustment range matters more than the motor or the looks: the Baratza Encore ESP divides its dial into a dedicated espresso range (settings 1–20, with close micro-steps) and a filter range (21–40), so a one-click change is small enough to fix a fast shot without overshooting. The Fellow Opus 2 takes a different route with a stepless side dial and 48 settings across the whole range, while the Eureka Specialita uses stepless micrometric adjustment — no clicks at all, just a smooth dial — paired with a touchscreen that doses by time.

Retention and static are the two quiet frustrations of home grinding. Retention is the ground coffee left inside the grinder after each dose; static makes fine grounds cling to the chute and spray across the counter. The Encore ESP tackles both with an anti-static dosing cup that drops grounds cleanly into the portafilter, and the Opus 2 adds an ioniser to cut static. These details sound minor until you grind every morning — then they decide whether the ritual feels calm or chaotic.

Dialling In Espresso: Grind Size, Dose and Freshness

Start with a dose: 18 grams of beans for a double shot is the standard starting point, and the Encore ESP's 24-gram dosing cup or the Specialita's timed dosing both make that repeatable. Grind, distribute the grounds evenly in the basket, tamp level with firm, even pressure, and pull the shot while timing it. Aim for roughly 36 grams of liquid espresso in 25–30 seconds. Faster and sour? Grind finer. Slower and bitter? Grind coarser. Change one variable at a time — grind size first — and keep notes for a few days. Most beginners fix their espresso within a week of this routine.

Freshness matters as much as grind. Coffee beans are at their best roughly 1–4 weeks after roasting, and grinding releases the aromatics immediately — ground coffee loses its best character within hours, not days. That is the real case for a grinder: not the gadget, but the habit of grinding each dose fresh. Store beans in an airtight container away from light, buy in small bags you will finish within two weeks, and never store beans in the freezer for daily use — the condensation on thawing does more harm than good. Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

  1. Espresso-range steps: the grinder must adjust finely enough in the espresso zone — micro-steps or stepless, not wide clicks.
  2. Burr size and type: 40 mm conical is the proven minimum; 48–55 mm burrs grind faster and more evenly.
  3. Dosing method: a dosing cup, timed dosing or a portafilter cradle — pick the workflow you will actually repeat.
  4. Retention and static: look for anti-static features and easy burr access for cleaning.
  5. Noise: if you brew before the household wakes, a quieter grinder like the Specialita is worth the premium.
  6. Parts and support: Baratza and Eureka both sell spare burrs and parts — a grinder should last years, not one warranty period.
