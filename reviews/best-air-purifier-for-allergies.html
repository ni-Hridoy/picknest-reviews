The single most important rule in this category is simple: only True HEPA counts. A filter labelled "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" is not the same thing and is not held to the same standard. True HEPA is rated to capture 99.97% of particles 0.3 microns and larger, which covers pollen, dust mite waste, most pet dander and mold spores. Every purifier in this roundup uses True HEPA or Blueair's equivalent HEPASilent filtration, so you can compare them on performance rather than marketing claims.

The second rule is to shop by CADR, not by the square footage printed on the box. CADR — Clean Air Delivery Rate — is independently verified by AHAM and measures how much clean air the machine actually produces. A good rule of thumb is that the dust or pollen CADR should be at least two-thirds of your room's area in square feet. Pet owners consistently rate it higher for smell control, and its washable pre-filter saves money over time. The trade-off is a slightly higher price and slightly higher annual filter costs, around $80 a year for the replacement set. If your main complaint is dust and pollen, buy the Coway. Noise, Filter Costs and Running Your Purifier Right

Noise is the factor most buyers underestimate. A purifier on its highest setting can reach 55–60 decibels — about the level of a normal conversation — while sleep mode on the Coway drops to around 24 decibels, quieter than a whisper. The practical lesson is to buy a purifier rated for a larger room than you need, so you can run it on low or medium and still get enough air changes. Allergy guidance generally aims for 4 to 5 air changes per hour in the bedroom, which a 360-square-foot-rated purifier can deliver in a 200-square-foot bedroom on a quiet setting.

Filter costs are the real long-term expense. Budget roughly $50 a year for the Coway, $80 for the Winix, and $70–$100 for the larger Blueair and Honeywell models. Skipping replacements is the most common mistake in this category: a clogged filter drops CADR sharply and can make the purifier louder as the fan works harder. Most models have a filter-change indicator; treat it as a deadline, not a suggestion. Pre-filters that can be washed or vacuumed, like those on the Coway and Winix, extend the life of the expensive HEPA element.

Placement and habits matter as much as the machine. Put the purifier in the bedroom, where you spend eight hours breathing the same air, and run it continuously rather than in bursts. Keep it a few feet from the wall with the intake unobstructed, and close the windows during high-pollen hours — a purifier cannot keep up with an open window in spring. If you use the ionizer or PlasmaWave feature, consider switching it off if anyone in the household has asthma, since these features can produce trace ozone. The mechanical HEPA filtration does the real work on its own.

Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

  1. True HEPA only: Avoid "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" filters — they are not held to the 99.97% standard.
  2. CADR: Dust or pollen CADR should be at least two-thirds of your room's square footage.
  3. Room size: Size up — a larger-rated purifier runs quieter while delivering enough air changes.
  4. Filter cost: Budget $50–$100 a year and check replacement prices before buying.
  5. Noise: Check the decibel rating on the lowest setting if the purifier will run in a bedroom.
  6. Certifications: AHAM Verified and CARB certification are meaningful quality signals.
  7. Ionizer: Make sure it can be switched off, especially if anyone has asthma.
