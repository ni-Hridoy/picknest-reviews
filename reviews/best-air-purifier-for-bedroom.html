What Is CADR, and How Does It Work in a Bedroom?

CADR — clean air delivery rate — is the volume of filtered air a purifier delivers, in cubic feet per minute, measured separately for smoke, dust and pollen. Smoke particles are the smallest and hardest to catch, so read the smoke figure first: it is the strictest test of the machine. The EPA's sizing chart assumes an 8-foot ceiling and says a 100 sq ft room needs a minimum CADR of 65 cfm, a 200 sq ft room needs 130, and a 300 sq ft room needs 195. That is the two-thirds rule in table form, and it is the only sizing method in this guide that comes from a regulator rather than a manufacturer.

Two bedroom-specific traps are worth knowing. First, CADR is measured at the purifier's highest speed, and a bedroom purifier spends most of its life on sleep mode. A machine that covers 358 sq ft on turbo may cover only about 210 sq ft at a quiet speed, so buy more CADR than your room strictly needs — the margin is what keeps the air clean overnight without noise. Second, ceiling height matters: the EPA table assumes 8 feet, and a bedroom with a 10-foot ceiling holds 25% more air, so the same machine covers less floor. Measure the room, multiply length by width, and compare against the smoke CADR — not the box coverage. Bedroom Air Quality: Humidity, Dust and Sleep

An air purifier is only one half of bedroom air. The EPA's guidance is clear that portable cleaners filter a single room and cannot remove all pollutants — gases and odours need activated carbon, and no CADR rating covers gas removal at all. If your bedroom air feels dry in winter, a purifier will not help; you need humidity. Our best humidifier for bedroom guide covers the machines that pair with a purifier for exactly this reason, and the combination — purifier for particles, humidifier for dry air — is the standard setup for allergy-prone sleepers.

Placement matters as much as the purchase. Put the purifier where air can circulate — not jammed against the wall or behind the bed — and keep it running overnight on sleep or auto mode rather than switching it off at bedtime. Particles and allergens rebuild within hours in a closed room, so the purifier earns its keep precisely while you sleep. Keep the bedroom door closed overnight so the machine filters one room instead of the hallway, change the filter on schedule (a clogged filter is a loud, inefficient filter), and vacuum the pre-filter regularly if it is washable. None of these picks has a CPSC recall on record through November 2026, which is worth checking for any purifier before you buy.
