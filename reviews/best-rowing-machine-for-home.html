Air vs Magnetic vs Water Resistance: Which Feel Is Right for You

Resistance type is the most important choice in a rowing machine, and it shapes the feel of every stroke. Air resistance, used by the Concept2 RowErg, works with a flywheel and fan: the harder you pull, the more resistance you get. That is why it feels like rowing on water and why it is the standard for training and competition. The trade-off is noise — a fan is audible, especially at high effort — and a slightly industrial look that some buyers love and others do not.

Magnetic resistance, used by the Sunny Health & Fitness SF-RW5515, is the quietest and smoothest option. Resistance is set by moving magnets closer to the flywheel, usually in levels on a dial. It is ideal for apartments, early-morning sessions and shared homes, and it needs almost no maintenance. The feel is more even than air resistance and less like real water, but for general fitness that rarely matters. Many magnetic rowers also fold compactly, which suits small spaces.

Water resistance sits in the middle: a tank of water with paddles creates a natural, swishing resistance that rises with effort, and the sound is genuinely pleasant. Water rowers look beautiful in a living room, but they are heavier, cost more, and need occasional water treatment to stay clean. Connected rowers like the Hydrow Wave use electromagnetic resistance, which lets the machine adjust resistance automatically during classes — a clever system, but one that ties the experience to a subscription. If you are unsure, air is the safest all-round choice, magnetic is the quietest, and water is the most atmospheric.

Monitors, Classes and the New Year Fitness Angle

The monitor is the difference between a machine you use and a machine that gathers dust. The Concept2 PM5 monitor is plain-looking but superb: it shows pace, watts, stroke rate and heart rate, and it connects to apps like ErgData for structured training. It needs no subscription, and the data it produces is the standard that coaches use worldwide. If you are self-motivated and like tracking progress, it is all you need.

Connected rowers take the opposite approach. The Hydrow Wave streams live and on-demand classes with instructors who coach your form and push your pace, and its compact design suits homes where a full-size rower would dominate the room. The NordicTrack RW700 offers a similar idea through iFIT, with a 10-inch touchscreen and workouts filmed in real locations around the world. Both are genuinely motivating — and both charge a monthly subscription after any trial period. Budget for that ongoing cost when you compare prices: a $1,500 rower plus $40 a month is a very different commitment from a $950 rower with no fees.

Buying Guide: What to Check Before You Buy

  1. Resistance type: Air for training feel, magnetic for quiet apartments, water for natural feel.
  2. Rail length: Check the maximum user height — tall rowers need a longer rail.
  3. Monitor: Look for pace, watts and stroke rate, plus app or heart-rate connectivity.
  4. Storage: Confirm it folds or splits, and measure the stored footprint.
  5. Max user weight: A higher rating signals a stronger frame and rail.
  6. Subscription: Add up the monthly class cost over a year before buying a connected rower.
  7. Warranty: Look for at least 2 years on parts, longer on the frame.
